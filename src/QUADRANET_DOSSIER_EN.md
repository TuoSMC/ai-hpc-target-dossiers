## 1. Bottom line

QuadraNet is a Los Angeles bare-metal, colocation, InfraCloud/private-cloud and DDoS-mitigation provider founded in 2001 by Ilan Mishan, operating AS8100 out of the Telecom Center building at 530 W 6th Street downtown plus leased space in roughly a dozen third-party facilities across the US and one in the Netherlands. **The commercial verdict is: do not forward-sell this account** — it is a credit-risk, asset-tracking and successor-account file, not a server pipeline, because the company has effectively collapsed (sold by VSS Capital Partners to Edge Centres on 2024-04-08; the Australian parent Edge Centres Pty Ltd had a liquidator appointed 2025-06-16; 200,000+ IPv4 addresses sold to HostPapa in early 2025; LA colocation customers given roughly one week to vacate in February 2025). The single strongest piece of evidence in this file is the hardware confirmation: QuadraNet's own promotional spec sheets state the chassis vendor verbatim — "1U Supermicro Server – 4x Hot-Swap Bays" on all four published LA dedicated-server SKUs, and "Supermicro CSE" chassis with "Supermicro IPMI" on its own bare-metal specials page ([LowEndBox exclusive offers](https://lowendbox.com/blog/quadranet-exclusive-black-friday-dedi-offers-in-los-angeles/)) — so Supermicro is a confirmed incumbent platform here, first-party and verbatim. The one thing that kills any deal is the unresolved legal and operating status of the US entity after the parent's liquidation: nobody has established whether QuadraNet Enterprises, LLC and Edge Centres LLC (US) are in receivership, dissolved, on-sold or nominally trading, and until that is answered no selling hours and no credit terms of any kind should be committed.

## 10. Cost ceiling

### 10.1 Assumption band — stated as assumptions, not as findings

> **THE PRICES ARE NOT LIVE.** [quadranet.com](https://quadranet.com/) serves a 445-byte black page today; there is no live catalogue to sample. Every price in §10.2 is taken from the **LAST KNOWN LIVE catalogue**, archived **2025-03-06** (servers) and **2024-12-07** (colocation) — one day before, and three months before, the operator's customer-eviction deadline respectively. **Treat them as the final observed price book of a failing operator, which is a floor, not a market rate.**

> **OPERATING-COST DEDUCTION — THESE ARE INDUSTRY-NORM ASSUMPTIONS BEING IMPOSED, NOT RESEARCHED FACTS ABOUT QUADRANET.** No QuadraNet financial statements, no power contract, no bandwidth cost, no rack cost and no headcount figure were found. **Nothing below was verified against any QuadraNet document.**

**Assumption band applied (ASSUMPTION):** total non-hardware cost — power, cooling, bandwidth, rack/space, IP space, remote hands, NOC, support, billing, churn and SG&A — consumes **45% to 60%** of gross monthly revenue for low-end US dedicated-server hosting of this class. The **MIDPOINT of 52.5%** is used, giving a **contribution margin factor of 0.475** applied uniformly. Every ceiling figure in §10.2 is: **`monthly_price × 0.475 × payback_months`**.

Component reasoning behind that band — **all of it assumption**:

- **Power (ASSUMPTION):** 150–300W average draw per 1U node; at PUE ~1.6 and a Los Angeles all-in commercial rate of roughly **$0.12–0.18/kWh**, approximately **$15–45/mo per node**. LA is a high-power-cost market, which pushes this to the top of the band.
- **Bandwidth (ASSUMPTION):** 10TB/mo included transfer. At low-end blended transit and IX pricing, roughly **$5–20/mo per node**, assuming typical low actual utilisation against the included cap.
- **Rack and space (ASSUMPTION):** 1U amortised out of a 42RU cabinet. Their own published cabinet price of $849–$999/mo implies roughly **$20–24 per RU per month at retail**; internal cost is lower, assumed **$8–15/U**.
- **IPv4 (ASSUMPTION):** a /29 is 8 addresses. Post-2024 lease market roughly **$0.50–0.70 per IP per month**, so **$4–6/mo per node**. This is a real and rising cost and a large part of why the address space was the first thing sold.
- **Support, NOC, provisioning, billing, churn and overhead (ASSUMPTION):** **20–30% of revenue**. Their catalogue promises 24×7×365 on-site engineers, free KVM attachment, automated OS installs and a 99.999% network SLA — an expensive service promise at a $109/mo price point.

**Sensitivity:** if the true opex ratio is **45%** rather than 52.5%, **every ceiling rises about 16%**. If it is **60%**, **every ceiling falls about 16%**. **The ranking between SKUs is unaffected either way, and the ranking is what matters for the pitch.**

**Further caveats (all assumptions, all stated as such):** (1) Ceilings assume **100% occupancy** for the full payback period, which is generous — real utilisation on a low-end fleet with monthly churn is materially below that, so treat these as **optimistic upper bounds**. (2) Ceilings are **pre-tax, pre-financing** and ignore the time value of money. (3) They **exclude switching, routing, out-of-band, cabling and DDoS-scrubbing capex**, all shared infrastructure the node must also carry. (4) The $999 colocation line is a **promotional rate that steps to $1,299 in months 7–12**, and the annual revenue figure holds the $999 rate flat, which **understates** the true 12-month take. (5) **The colocation ceiling is not comparable to the server ceilings** — for colocation the operator buys rack, power and network, not servers.

| **BOM C** — $649/mo top-of-range all-SSD (2× E5-2620v4, 128GB, 4× 960GB enterprise SSD) | **$2,102.90** (CPUs 13.98 + board 151.99 + RAM 663.99 + SSDs 1,139.96 + PSU 37.99 + NIC 0 + rails 94.99) | $3,699.30 | $5,548.95 | $7,398.60 | **6.8 months** | **$1,596.40** |

**What the gap actually means for the pitch**

1. **The CPU is economically free, and that is the whole story.** A pair of Xeon E5-2620v4 processors — the silicon behind the $379, $419, $499 and $649 SKUs, which is to say **the top half of this operator's entire price book** — costs **$13.98**. Fourteen dollars of CPU generating **$4,548 to $7,788** of annual revenue. Against that, **no new-silicon platform can win on payback arithmetic**, and no amount of performance-per-watt argument survives contact with a 2.7-month payback. **THIS is why the catalogue froze for 74 months: refusing to refresh was not neglect, it was the rational answer to the economics in front of them.**

2. **The cost has migrated entirely out of compute and into memory and flash.** In BOM C, CPU is **0.7%** of the bill of materials while RAM is **31.6%** and SSD is **54.2%** — together **85.8%**. In BOM B, RAM alone is **31.5%** and the two CPUs are **2.8%**. **Any pitch aimed at this buyer that leads with processor generation is aimed at the smallest line on the invoice.** The lever that moves their P&L is **DIMM and NVMe cost per usable GB, and density** — how much billable memory and flash fits in a rack-unit and a kilowatt.

3. **Self-assembly is genuinely cheap at the top of the range and genuinely expensive at the bottom.** BOM B pays back in **2.7 months**; BOM A takes **9.8 months** and has only **18% headroom** in year one — because a **$207.99 motherboard is being amortised against a $109/mo rent**, and the board alone is **41% of the build**. **That inversion is the opening.** An integrated platform cannot beat $13.98 of used Broadwell on price, but it does not have to: it has to beat **the entry-tier build**, where the fixed cost of board, PSU, rails, chassis, IPMI licensing, burn-in labour, RMA handling and spares pooling is spread across a rent line too thin to carry it. **Pitch density and per-node fixed-cost elimination at the bottom of the range, not raw performance at the top.**

4. **The unpriced items are where an integrated platform actually earns its margin — and they are missing from every self-assembly spreadsheet this buyer builds.** The BOM totals **EXCLUDE the bare chassis** (rails sourced at $94.99, no verified chassis price) and **exclude every labour and lifecycle cost**: assembly, firmware qualification, burn-in, imaging, on-site racking, spares inventory, RMA logistics and the engineering time to keep four different CPU generations bootable from one provisioning system. A shop running **E-2124G, E-2146G, E5-2650 and E5-2620v4 side by side** is carrying **four BIOS baselines, three memory types (DDR3 RDIMM, DDR4 RDIMM, DDR4 UDIMM) and two socket families**. Consolidating to one platform is worth real money that never appears in a parts comparison — **that is the integrated-platform argument, and it is an operational-cost argument, not a price argument.**

5. **Read against the failure itself:** the arithmetic above says the fleet was **profitable per node** and still the company **could not cover $599,379.76 of rent**. That means the binding constraint was **never hardware cost** — it was **occupancy, revenue per rack-unit and fixed facility cost**. An operator getting $379/mo for a box whose parts cost $491.92 is not losing money on hardware; it is losing money on **empty racks and a downtown Los Angeles lease signed in 2004 and 2010**. **Sell revenue-per-rack and revenue-per-kilowatt to this segment. Selling cheaper servers to a hosting company with an occupancy problem solves the wrong equation.**

### Classification: **NOT A FORWARD-SELLING TARGET** — credit risk and asset tracking only

Supermicro is a **confirmed, first-party incumbent platform** at this account — but the account itself has collapsed. The pre-sales warning is the whole angle:

**There is no AI upsell hook** — QuadraNet never had a single GPU product, so the usual accelerated-compute wedge does not exist here. **The only realistic commercial angle is asset succession**: who now controls the physical Supermicro estate in the LA suites, and whether the successor (Edge Centres' remaining US assets, HostPapa which took the IP space, or whoever takes over the suites) becomes a new account in its own right.

**Do not spend selling hours, and do not extend credit terms in any form, until the legal status of the US entity is established.** If the account is registered at all, register it as an asset-tracking record with the credit-risk note attached.

### The qualifying question to ask on first contact

> **"Which legal entity currently controls the datacenter suites — and the equipment inside them — at 530 W 6th Street and 6171 W Century Blvd?"**

**This question is put to the asset successor, not to QuadraNet.** It is the single question that decides whether there is anything here at all.

### Rule 8 distributor caution — read before anyone dials

QuadraNet ran a sizeable fleet across roughly a dozen facilities, an ASN with ~300,000 IPv4 addresses at peak, and 1,000+ active customers — **yet it has never once appeared in the CRM (verified 2026-08-03: no lead, no account, no do-not-call record).** The same pattern holds across the whole bare-metal layer studied. The only reasonable inference is that **these providers bought their servers through distribution, not direct from the manufacturer.**

**Under Rule 8, a distributor may cross territory boundaries, but only with advance approval and a "do not call" flag applied.** So the sequence is fixed: **confirm the channel position first** — establish whether QuadraNet (or its successor) is already an existing account of a distributor and obtain the cross-territory approval plus do-not-call flag — **then register the lead, and only then make contact.** Reversing that order creates channel conflict. For this provider specifically, the channel check is cheap and should happen anyway, because it may also reveal who holds the outstanding receivable and therefore who controls the hardware.
