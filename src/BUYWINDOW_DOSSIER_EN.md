## 1. How to read this

### A window is not a trigger, and the difference decides what you do this week

**A WINDOW is a dated period** in which an operator is likely to commit capital, derived from an observed purchase cadence and anchored to a named, dated event — a stock announcement, a catalogue SKU first appearing in an archive snapshot, a press release with a dateline, a registry timestamp. Windows go in the calendar in Section 2. Every one of them can be traced back through the "why now" cell to a source you can open.

**A TRIGGER is an observable event with no date attached** — a funding close, a facility Ready-for-Service confirmation, an unannounced IP block being routed, a stalled product code gaining a price, a vacant seat being filled. Operators whose buying is gated on a trigger rather than a calendar go in the **event-gated** table immediately after the calendar, with the trigger named and the exact surface to watch. **No month is invented for them.** If the dossier says the clock is event-driven, this document says so and stops there.

**Three operators have both**, and the distinction matters commercially:

- **Hot Aisle** — the *call* is dated and urgent (2026-08, anchored to the 2026-07-14 price increase and the live investors page); the *order* is gated on the close of their $50–100M raise. Engage before the raise closes, because the platform decision is made while the investor model is being built, not after the money lands.
- **phoenixNAP** — the *window* is dated H2 2026 – H1 2027 off a 9–12 month CPU cadence; the *GPU decision specifically* is gated on a product code that has sat unpriced for sixteen months.
- **OpenMetal** — the *window* is dated Q4 2026 – Q2 2027 off a published expansion intention; the *forcing event* is a vacant CRO seat being filled.

They appear in both tables, and each row says which half it is.

### Restate the assumption discipline — every time, without exception

Every "hardware ceiling" in this document answers one question: **what is the most this operator can pay for the hardware and still recover it inside N months of full occupancy?** The formula is:

> **ceiling = monthly rent × (1 − operating-cost deduction) × N months**

**The operating-cost deduction is an ASSUMPTION in every single case.** Not one of these eleven companies publishes cost or margin data. Ten are private and file no public accounts; the eleventh (Sharon AI) is SEC-reporting but discloses no per-node cost structure. The deductions below are industry-norm judgements chosen by the analyst, not researched facts, and **they must be restated as assumptions in any customer-facing use.**

**Also excluded from every ceiling, all of which bias the numbers optimistically:** cost of capital (a 24-month payback at a realistic 10–12% is ~8–10% more demanding than shown); occupancy below 100%; residual value; assembly labour, burn-in, licensing, rails, freight, spares and RMA pool; and sales, support and G&A allocation. **Payback windows of 12/18/24 months are hurdle rates, not asset-life constraints** — hardware in this segment is routinely held four to six years, and Database Mart is still earning revenue on 2016 Broadwell today.

**Three further disciplines that apply throughout:**

1. **"UNVERIFIED — portal blocked" never means unfinanced.** Nine of the eleven UCC verdicts are unverified. An empty filing set means *nothing was seen*, not that nothing exists. None of these nine may be described to a customer, to credit, or in CRM as having "no liens found."
2. **All BOM prices are retail or spot street prices.** These operators buy at distributor, broker, secondary-market or programme pricing — plausibly 15–40% below street on CPUs and drives, and far below on memory if they hold allocation. **Every BOM total is an UPPER BOUND.** The direction of each conclusion is robust; the magnitudes compress under real contract pricing.
3. **Chassis and barebone prices are missing from most BOMs**, typically 25–45% of a refurb build. Every affected total therefore *understates* true assembled cost, and every payback figure is correspondingly optimistic — meaning the gap against an integrated platform is *wider* than shown, not narrower.
