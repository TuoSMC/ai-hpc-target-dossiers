## 1. Bottom line

WebNX, Inc. is a privately held, founder-led dedicated-server, colocation and GPU/AI hosting provider headquartered inside its own flagship datacenter at 119 North 600 West, Bldg 3B, Ogden, Utah, with leased footprints in Los Angeles (Equinix LA3), New York, Salt Lake City and Dallas, running AS18450 and self-describing "thousands of high-end servers globally" on a staff of 11–50 ([LinkedIn company page](https://www.linkedin.com/company/webnx-inc-), self-reported; [BBB profile](https://www.bbb.org/us/ut/ogden/profile/internet-service/webnx-inc-1166-90026506), file opened 2020-06-03). Commercially this is a **platform-conversion target, not an incumbent-defence account** — WebNX is a confirmed component-level whitebox integrator that has never named a system vendor anywhere in public, sits inside Team 1's own territory, and has never once appeared in the CRM. The single strongest piece of evidence is WebNX's own publicly priced flagship AI SKU — **dual AMD EPYC 9575F (128C/256T), 1.5 TB DDR5, 2 × 15 TB Gen5 NVMe, 4 × NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 96 GB at $7,999/month** ([Ogden GPU & AI store](https://clients.webnx.com/index.php?rp=/store/ogden-gpu-and-ai-servers), read 2026-08-03) — which proves they already buy and resell current-generation Turin + Blackwell platforms, while the same catalogue still carries 2017-silicon V100s and consumer RTX 3090s with **zero H100 / H200 / L40S coverage**. The one thing that could kill the deal is the channel: a fleet this size with no CRM record at all points at purchase through distribution, so under Rule 8 the distributor position must be cleared *before* the first call, or the engagement becomes a channel conflict rather than a sale.

## 10. Cost ceiling

What each server can be worth to WebNX, and what the same box costs to build today. This section answers one question: **at what hardware cost does a WebNX SKU still pay for itself?**

### 10.1 Assumptions — read these first, they are assumptions and not findings

**OPERATING-COST DEDUCTION: 40% of gross monthly rent, applied uniformly.** Ceiling = `monthly_price × (1 − 0.40) × N months`. **THIS IS AN ASSUMPTION, NOT A RESEARCHED FACT** — it is an industry-norm band selected for this analysis, not something WebNX disclosed. **Treat every ceiling figure below as a modelled estimate.**

- **Assumption band.** 30% (low) to 50% (high) is the defensible range for power + bandwidth + rack + remote hands + IP + churn/idle allowance on a single dedicated server. At 30% opex the 24-month ceilings **rise by 17%** versus the figures given; at 50% they **fall by 17%**. Applied to the EPYC 9255 SKU, the 24-month ceiling spans **$10,062 (30%) / $8,626 (40%, used) / $7,188 (50%)**.
- **Why 40% is the midpoint, and the one real anchor.** WebNX published its own **retail** colocation rate card on 2025-01-24 ([WHT thread 1933969](https://www.webhostingtalk.com/showthread.php?t=1933969)): a 1U/2U single server with 2 Amps at 208V (~400W), a /29, a 10G port and 50 TB of bandwidth retails at **$129/month**; a full rack with 3 KW usable, 100G and 100 TB is **$1,500/month**, with extra power at $300/kW; a private 25-rack cage with 75 KW pooled is **$23,000/month**. That $129 is a **retail price to a third party** and therefore an **upper bound** on WebNX's own internal cost to house one machine — their true internal cost is materially lower, particularly in Ogden where they operate their own ~100,000–120,000 sq ft facility rather than renting. Against a $259/mo dedicated SKU that retail-equivalent housing cost is ~50%; against a $599/mo SKU it is ~22%. **40% sits between those and is deliberately conservative.**
- **Site asymmetry — real, but not modelled.** Ogden is owner-operated, so opex there should sit at the **low** end of the band. Los Angeles is leased space at Equinix LA3, where WebNX self-describes as "one of the largest clients… now over 200 racks and 5 cages" on a recently renewed 5-year term; opex there belongs at the **high** end. A single 40% was applied to all sites **for comparability**. A site-split model would raise Ogden ceilings and lower LA ceilings by roughly 10 percentage points each.
- **What is excluded.** No cost of capital / discounting (a 24-month payback at a realistic 10–12% cost of capital is **~8–10% more demanding** than the undiscounted figure shown); no sales, support, licensing or overhead allocation; no allowance for the fact that `stocklevel=1` on **26 of 57 live SKUs** implies these are one-off builds without volume procurement leverage; no residual-value credit at end of life, which for used-Xeon and storage chassis is genuinely non-trivial and would **raise** the effective ceiling.
- **Payback-window choice.** 12 / 18 / 24 months. Note that dedicated-server hardware in this segment is routinely held **4–6 years** — the 2023 catalogue was still selling Xeon E5-2696v2 (2013 silicon) — so **a 24-month payback is a commercial hurdle rate, not an asset-life constraint**. Read the 24-month column as "the price at which this SKU is comfortably profitable", **not** "the price above which it is impossible".

### 10.4 Roll-up — and what the gap means for the pitch

**HEADLINE: at today's street prices, WebNX's flagship current-gen EPYC SKU cannot be rebuilt at anything close to its own asking price. The gap is entirely memory.**

**SKU A — pid 587, AMD EPYC 9255 / 768 GB DDR5 / 2 × 3.84 TB NVMe, $599/mo, Ogden.**
Assembled component cost at 2026-08-10 street: **$34,637.48** (CPU $2,835.00 + RAM $24,768.00 + NVMe $5,140.00 + board $649.00 + NIC $257.49 + chassis/PSU $987.99). Rent-derived 24-month ceiling: **$8,626**. **BOM is 4.02× the ceiling.** Undiscounted payback at 40% opex: **96.4 months — eight years.** Memory alone is **$24,768, which is 287% of the entire 24-month ceiling**; the 768 GB of DDR5 costs 2.9× what the whole server can generate in two years. Now the same build at **pre-crisis component prices** (64 GB DDR5 RDIMM at ~$255, 3.84 TB NVMe at ~$500, everything else unchanged): **$8,789.48 — 1.02× the 24-month ceiling, payback 24.5 months.** That is a textbook, healthy whitebox build. **So the $599 price point was set in a world where the machine paid for itself in ~24 months, and that world ended.** Roughly $255 → $2,064 per 64 GB module is an **~8× move**.

**SKU B — pid 884, Ryzen 9950X / 128 GB / 7.6 TB / RTX 5090, $479/mo, Los Angeles.**
Assembled component cost: **$11,679.47** (CPU $499.99 + RAM $1,094.00 + NVMe $5,140.00 + GPU $3,400.00 + board ~$300 + NIC $257.49 + chassis/PSU $987.99). 24-month ceiling: **$6,898. BOM is 1.69×. Payback 40.6 months.** The RTX 5090 alone at $3,400 is **49% of the 24-month ceiling** — and it is a consumer card with no ECC, sold into an AI-inference use case. Note the compounding squeeze: GPU street price is running **~70% above the $1,999 MSRP** because of GDDR7 costs, so this SKU is hit by the same DRAM shortage **twice**, once in system memory and once in the GPU.

**SKU C — pid 942, Dual Xeon Gold 6226 / 192 GB DDR4 / 1.2 TB NVMe, $259/mo, Los Angeles.**
Assembled component cost: **$2,467.03** (CPUs $147.84 + RAM $1,211.70 + NVMe ~$200 + board ~$350 + NIC $257.49 + chassis/PSU ~$300). 24-month ceiling: **$3,730. BOM is 0.66× — the ONLY one of the three that clears its ceiling. Payback 15.9 months.** The two 12-core Xeons cost $147.84, **less than the network card**. Even here, refurbished DDR4 is 49% of the BOM.

**What the gap means for pitching an integrated platform against self-assembly:**

1. **Do not lead with integration savings on chassis, boards or CPUs.** Those line items are $1,900–2,000 combined on a $34,637 build — **under 6%**. A whitebox operator with its own hands in Ogden and Flex Space at LA3 will always beat an integrator on assembly labour, and they know it. Any pitch built on "we save you integration effort" **loses on arithmetic**.
2. **Lead with memory procurement, because that is where 71% of the current-gen BOM sits.** The credible value propositions are **contracted DDR5 allocation at below-spot pricing, a firm forward price, or memory-inclusive system pricing** that shelters them from spot volatility. An integrated platform that bundles memory at contract rather than spot is worth **multiples of every other saving combined**.
3. **The self-assembly route is now actively worse than it used to be for them, and that is the opening.** A whitebox builder's structural edge is buying components on the open market — which is precisely the market that broke. **A vendor with allocation has a temporary, real advantage over a spot buyer for the first time in this company's 27-year history.**
4. **Match the pitch to the tier.** The used-Xeon tier (SKU C) still works economically and needs nothing from us. **The current-gen EPYC tier (SKU A) is where the model is broken and where the conversation should start.** The GPU tier (SKU B) is the middle case — squeezed but survivable.
5. **Expect a financing conversation, not just a price conversation.** At 4.02× the 24-month ceiling, no rational operator funds SKU A from cash — which is exactly why the purchase clock stopped on 2025-10-12 (§9) and why the live catalogue has fallen from 141 SKUs to 57. The deal that unlocks this account is most likely **structured** (extended terms, lease, or memory-inclusive bundling), **which is why the UCC lien position in §8 must be cleared first** — it determines whether structured terms are even available.
6. **Sanity-check on their pricing power:** at spot memory, a rebuilt SKU A would need roughly **$2,400/mo** to hit a 24-month payback versus the **$599** they charge today. They are not going to quadruple list prices. Something else has to give — **allocation, financing, or configuration** (smaller memory footprints per box). **Watch for memory downsizing in new SKUs as the leading indicator of which lever they chose.**

## 14. Supermicro sales angle

### Classification: **Whitebox integrator → platform conversion**

**This is NOT an incumbent-defence account.** Supermicro's presence in the WebNX fleet is graded **unverified** — no chassis, no motherboard, no system-vendor name appears anywhere in WebNX's public material. **Opening with "we know you're running Supermicro" will destroy credibility on the first sentence.** Turn it into a question instead.

**The honest wedge:**

WebNX buys at the **component and barebones level** and integrates in-house — that is confirmed by its own job posting ("assembling & disassembling servers, testing hardware," "repair or replace faulty hardware components," "hundreds of server configurations and parts each year," [Indeed](https://www.indeed.com/viewjob?jk=9329bfea43788efd)). And they have **already proven they will buy top-end current-generation AI platforms**: dual EPYC 9575F + 4 × RTX PRO 6000 96 GB, publicly listed at **$7,999/month** ([Ogden GPU & AI store](https://clients.webnx.com/index.php?rp=/store/ogden-gpu-and-ai-servers)). Yet the same catalogue still carries **2017-era V100** and **2020 consumer RTX 3090** nodes, and offers **no H100, H200, L40S or GB200 at all**.

The angle: **GPU-optimised 4U/5U barebones for V100 and RTX 3090 replacement, plus additional dual-Turin 4-GPU and 8-GPU platforms.** There is also a natural product fit that Dell and HPE structurally cannot match — WebNX sells **AM5 Ryzen 9950X/9950X3D and LGA1851 Core Ultra 9 285K** platforms, and Dell and HPE do not build servers around those desktop-class sockets while Supermicro does.

Secondary hooks worth holding in reserve:
- The **full-rack colocation SKU ($1,199/mo) publishes no amperage allocation**, and no kW-per-rack density or PUE exists publicly — density is an unforced discovery topic that leads naturally into AI-platform sizing.
- The **April 2021 generator fire** during the Ogden city-wide outage is a legitimate resiliency conversation and a plausible driver of power-plant and refresh capex.
- **DGX Spark units are already being paired at 200 Gbps** — they are building small multi-node AI clusters and will hit interconnect and density limits.

### The one qualifying question for first contact

> **"For your AM5 and LGA1851 SKUs and your 4-GPU boxes — whose barebones or motherboards are you building on today?"**

Ask this and nothing else on the first call. **Do not quote. Do not assume an incumbent vendor.** One sentence resolves the highest-priority unknown in this account: whether Supermicro is already in WebNX's supply chain at all.

### Rule 8 — distributor caution (read before dialling)

WebNX runs a sizeable fleet — self-reported "thousands of high-end servers globally", 150+ racks in Equinix LA3, a 100,000+ sq ft self-operated Ogden facility, ~100,096 IPv4 addresses on AS18450 — **yet has never once appeared in the CRM** (salesleads Search, Type = All, verified 2026-08-03: no lead, no account, no do-not-call). The only reasonable inference is that **their servers are bought through distribution, not direct**. Corroborating behaviour: the job posting describes moving "pallets full of servers or other computer parts with a pallet jack" and testing "hundreds of server configurations and parts each year"; LowEndTalk carries threads titled "Regarding WebNX distributors."

**Under Rule 8 a distributor may cross territory boundaries, but this requires advance approval and a do-not-call flag.** Therefore, before anyone dials: **confirm with the channel contact whether WebNX is already an existing account of a distributor.** If it is, this shifts from new-business development to a distributor-managed existing account, and the opening, the quoting route and the registration method all change.

**Sequence — do not reorder:** ① clear the channel/distributor position (Rule 8) → ② register the lead (Ogden UT = West Coast South excl. CA = **T1 | T31**, Team 1's own territory, direct registration) → ③ make contact, asking only the qualifying question above.
