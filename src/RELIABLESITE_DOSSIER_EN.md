## 1. Bottom line

ReliableSite is a founder-owned, ~11–50-person U.S. bare-metal / dedicated-server provider running its own AS23470 network and its own servers across six leased colocation regions — Piscataway NJ, Newark NJ, Miami FL, Los Angeles CA, Amsterdam NL, and a Querétaro MX build-out — selling single-socket desktop/workstation-class machines at $49–$699/month. The commercial verdict is that this is a **confirmed Supermicro account that has already been half-lost**: the chassis business is gone to an in-house design, but the motherboard and power-supply socket is explicitly still open, and there is a clean, empty AI/GPU shelf to fill — so this is a board-level defence plus a greenfield GPU play, not a routine renewal, and it must be run by the East Coast team, not Team 1. The single strongest piece of evidence in the whole file is CEO Radic Davydov's on-record [LowEndBox interview](https://lowendbox.com/blog/custom-built-for-success-interview-with-radic-davydov-ceo-of-reliablesite/), in which he names Supermicro MicroCloud in his fleet and then explains, in his own words, that he designed a proprietary ~50%-higher-density chassis after MicroCloud replacement parts became unavailable and cost "2-3 times the cost" — while deliberately keeping it compatible with off-the-shelf motherboards and power supplies. The one thing that could kill the deal is opening with a vendor-integrated chassis or complete-system pitch: that re-opens the exact wound that lost the account, and the honest opening is the board/PSU socket and the missing GPU platform — the motherboard supplier behind the custom chassis is the single most commercially important unknown in this entire dossier.

### 9.2 Cadence

**Observed intervals.** Purchase events are defined strictly as the first-ever appearance of a new CPU or GPU generation in the catalogue across the twelve snapshots. Five qualify.

|---|---|---|
| **E1** | 2023-08-02 | Zen 4 mainstream 65W (Ryzen 7600 / 7700) plus first-ever GPU product (Quadro RTX 4000) |

| **E3** | 2024-12-18 | Zen 5 desktop (Ryzen 9900X / 9950X) plus Raptor Lake Refresh (i9-14900K) plus data-centre GPU (nVidia A10) — the largest single refresh observed |

Intervals between consecutive events, in months: **E1→E2 = 9.5 · E2→E3 = 7.0 · E3→E4 = 5.8 · E4→E5 = 3.9** (or 2.1 if E5 is dated to the August 2025 announcement rather than the catalogue sighting). **The sequence is monotonically decreasing — the refresh clock was accelerating steadily right up to the last event.**

**Median months.** **6.4 months** (median of 9.5, 7.0, 5.8, 3.9). Mean 6.6 months. Using the announcement-corrected E5 date the median falls to 6.4 and the mean to 6.1. **Call it a 6-month cadence with a tightening trend through 2025.**

**Last purchase event.** **AMD EPYC 4545P** (EPYC 4005 series, Zen 5, 16C/32T, 65W TDP, socket AM5). Announced in ReliableSite's own newsroom in **August 2025** for New York, Miami and Los Angeles; first observed in the catalogue in the **2025-10-08** snapshot at $149/mo for 128GB DDR5 + 2TB NVMe and $199/mo for 256GB DDR5 + 4TB NVMe. As of today, **2026-08-10**, that is **12 months since announcement and 10.1 months since catalogue debut, against a 6.4-month median** — roughly **1.6× to 1.9× their historical interval**, with no new silicon in four consecutive snapshots (2025-11, 2026-03, 2026-06, 2026-08). **This is the longest silicon drought in the observed window by a wide margin** ([newsroom post](https://www.reliablesite.net/hosting-news/offering-amd-epyc-4545p-dedicated-servers/)).

**Next window estimate.** **September 2026 to January 2027, with highest probability in October–November 2026.** Two forcing functions, pulling in the same direction. First, the refresh clock is already 4–6 months overdue and the backlog of un-adopted silicon is now substantial — Zen 5 X3D parts, the EPYC 4005 range above the 4545P, and NVIDIA's L4/L40S generation are all shipping while their catalogue tops out at a 65W 16-core and an Ampere-era A10 that has been their GPU flagship since December 2024. Second and more decisive, they are fitting out three new sites simultaneously: **Amsterdam went live 2026-04-29, and Dallas (DFW) plus Querétaro (QRO) availability flags appeared in the markup on 2026-07-30** with Mexico still at pre-order. New sites require new iron regardless of the refresh clock — **the Querétaro and Dallas fit-out is the buy**, and it has to land before those sites can convert pre-orders to revenue. The countervailing force is component pricing, which is why the drought exists at all; if DRAM and NAND stay at current levels the buy may be split into smaller staged tranches rather than one large order.

**Basis.** Twelve Wayback Machine captures of [reliablesite.net/dedicated-servers/](https://www.reliablesite.net/dedicated-servers/) spanning 2023-03-14 to 2026-08-07, parsed from the page's own structured markup, cross-checked against the live page on 2026-08-10 and against ReliableSite's own dated newsroom posts ([EPYC 4545P, August 2025](https://www.reliablesite.net/hosting-news/offering-amd-epyc-4545p-dedicated-servers/); [Amsterdam, April 2026](https://www.reliablesite.net/hosting-news/reliablesite-dedicated-servers-are-now-available-in-amsterdam/)). **First-appearance dates are UPPER BOUNDS constrained by snapshot cadence — the true purchase precedes the sighting.** The EPYC 4545P case calibrates that lag at roughly two months.

**Confidence.** **Medium-high** on the historical cadence itself (five events, consistent monotonic tightening, corroborated by an independent vendor announcement date). **Medium** on the next-window estimate — the direction is well supported by the three-site expansion, but the timing is hostage to a component market in genuine dislocation, and one snapshot (2024-05-18) was a thin capture that could have masked an intermediate event.

**GAP — coverage holes that bound this cadence work.**
- Wayback coverage of the catalogue between **2024-12-18 and 2025-06-10** is nearly absent — only one status-200 capture exists in that six-month span (2025-01-08 returned 403, 2025-09-19 a 301). **A purchase event in H1 2025 could have gone unobserved**, which would shorten the measured E3→E4 interval.
- The **2024-05-18** capture is a thin archive object (149,785 bytes against 200,000–380,000 for neighbours) yielding only 15 SKUs. Its counts are a floor.
- Live retrieval of the dedicated-servers page by direct HTTP client was blocked (5,639-byte response), so the "today" reading is taken from the 2026-08-07 Wayback capture cross-checked against a successful proxied fetch of the live page. The two agree on newest silicon and top-of-range pricing, but the live SKU count read 22 configurations versus 20 in the archived capture — **a small discrepancy consistent with three days of stock movement.**
- **Total server count, rack count, cabinet count and installed fleet size are nowhere disclosed.** Catalogue availability figures show only current unsold stock per SKU per site (typically 1–5 units), which says nothing about installed base. Any statement about how many servers they run is unsupported by this research.

## 10. Cost ceiling

### 10.1 Assumptions — read this before the table

**Method.** Hardware cost ceiling at N months = **(monthly rent − monthly operating cost) × N**. This is the maximum a rational operator can pay for the hardware and still recover it inside N months of full occupancy, before financing cost and before any return on capital. Annual revenue is stated as list rent × 12 at 100% occupancy.

> **EVERY OPERATING-COST FIGURE BELOW IS AN INDUSTRY-NORM ASSUMPTION, NOT A RESEARCHED FACT ABOUT RELIABLESITE.** None of it was sourced from the company. It is a defensible band for a self-operating bare-metal host at 1–5 Tbps scale with in-house staff, and **it must be restated as an assumption in any customer-facing use.**

|---|---|---|---|
| **Power and cooling** | Blended all-in electricity **$0.13/kWh** at **PUE 1.5**, applied to estimated *average* system draw (not nameplate TDP) → **$0.1423 per watt per month** ($0.13/kWh × 730 h/mo × 1.5) | $0.09–$0.18/kWh and PUE 1.3–1.7, which moves the power line roughly **±45%** | ASSUMPTION. **What is deliberately excluded, and which way it biases the ceilings.** Occupancy is assumed at **100%**; real-world utilisation of 70–85% would cut every ceiling by 15–30%, and the live page shows single-unit availability across most SKUs, which suggests they do run tight. Cost of capital, IP address lease cost (material given they lease most of their announced space), setup labour and RMA/spares are all excluded. **Every published ceiling is therefore an OPTIMISTIC UPPER BOUND. If a SKU already fails to clear its BOM at these ceilings, it fails harder in reality.**

**The one finding that survives any reasonable assumption set.** At $49/mo the Intel Special returns roughly **$7/mo of contribution** against a $42 opex assumption, giving a **12-month ceiling of $84** and a **24-month ceiling of $168**. No new hardware of any kind can be bought for that. The bottom of their range is only viable on fully depreciated iron — which is exactly what their own clearance page says it is, marketing previously-deployed servers with SuperMicro chassis and a 5+ year life expectancy. **That tier is structurally unaddressable for a new-hardware sale, and pitching into it will fail on arithmetic no matter how the assumptions are tuned.**

### 10.4 Roll-up — assembled street cost against the rent-derived ceiling

**BUILD A — AMD EPYC 4545P, 128GB DDR5, 2TB NVMe, sells at $199/mo.**
Assembled at street: CPU $592.99 + AS-1015A-MT barebone $1,046.00 + 4×32GB ECC UDIMM $861.36 + 2TB NVMe $385.00 = **$2,885.35**.
Against the rent-derived ceilings: **12-month $1,788 — FAILS by $1,097. 18-month $2,682 — FAILS by $203. 24-month $3,576 — clears with $691 of headroom.**
Read that carefully. **Their newest, highest-volume, deliberately-chosen-for-efficiency platform does not pay back its own street-price bill of materials inside eighteen months. It needs roughly twenty-two months.** And that is measured against an optimistic ceiling that assumes 100% occupancy and excludes cost of capital.

**BUILD B — AMD Ryzen 9950X, 256GB DDR5, 2×4TB NVMe, sells at $399/mo.** The only SKU stocked in all six data centres, and one of the two SKUs chosen to open Amsterdam.

Against the ceilings: **12-month $4,032 — FAILS by roughly $2,150. 18-month $6,048 — FAILS by roughly $134, essentially a dead heat. 24-month $8,064 — clears with roughly $1,880 of headroom.**
**Memory alone is 54% of this build. That single number is the whole story of their 2026.**

**BUILD C — nVidia A10 GPU node, 128GB DDR5, 2×2TB NVMe, sells at $699/mo.**
Assembled at street: A10 $2,750 (lowest listing; $4,299 at the median) + CPU approximately $330 (unverified — see GAP in §10.3) + H13SAE-MF $549.00 + 128GB ECC UDIMM $861.36 + 2×2TB NVMe $770.00 = **approximately $5,260, excluding a GPU-capable chassis that was not priced**.
Against the ceilings: **12-month $7,488 — CLEARS with roughly $2,228 of headroom even before counting the chassis. 18-month $11,232 and 24-month $14,976 — clears comfortably.** Even at the $4,299 median GPU price the build lands near $6,810 and still pays back inside twelve months.

#### What the gap means for pitching an integrated platform against self-assembly

1. **The self-assembly advantage has inverted, and 2026 is why.** From 2023 through late 2025 this company's whole model worked because component street prices fell faster than rents: across the catalogue, matched configurations got cheaper year after year — Ryzen 3700X 128GB $139 → $99, Ryzen 5950X 128GB $269 → $139, i9-13900K 128GB $299 → $179. Buying parts and screwing them together was straightforwardly the cheapest path. **Since March 2026 that reversed hard.** Every matched configuration in the catalogue is now rising: EPYC 4545P 128GB $149 → $199 (+34%), EPYC 4545P 256GB $199 → $279 (+40%), Ryzen 9950X 256GB $299 → $399 (+33%), Ryzen 9950X 192GB $249 → $329 (+32%), A10 GPU $599 → $699 (+17%), Ryzen 7950X 192GB $239 → $299 (+25%). **The increases scale with installed memory, which identifies DRAM as the driver.** They are passing component inflation straight through to customers, and the highest-memory SKUs are being repriced hardest.

2. **The arithmetic now argues for you rather than against you.** When memory and NAND were cheap, no integrator could beat a hoster buying DIMMs on the open market. Today a vendor with contracted DRAM and NAND supply and volume board pricing can land an integrated EPYC 4005 or Zen 5 node materially below the $2,885 street figure in Build A — and **the difference between an 18-month payback and a 22-month payback is exactly the difference between a system they can approve and one they defer.** That is the pitch: not performance, not density, not TCO in the abstract, but **pulling their payback back inside eighteen months on a build they currently cannot make work at street prices**.

3. **The corroborating tell that they have already hit this wall.** There has been no new CPU or GPU generation in their catalogue for four consecutive snapshots covering ten months, against a 6.4-month historical median (§9.2). The entire bulk-HDD storage line (32TB, 40TB, 80TB) disappeared between June and August 2026. The 2,048GB Threadripper flagship disappeared. Catalogue breadth collapsed from 40 SKU families in October 2025 to 17 in August 2026. **A hoster that is buying does not shrink its catalogue by 58% in ten months. They have stopped buying and started repricing — and they are doing it while simultaneously fitting out Amsterdam, Dallas and Querétaro, which is a contradiction that has to resolve in a purchase.**

4. **Lead with the GPU tier, and lead with it now.** Build C clears a twelve-month payback with roughly $2,200 to spare at the lowest street GPU price, and their A10 has been the GPU flagship since December 2024 — twenty months on an Ampere-generation part while L4 and L40S class accelerators ship. It is simultaneously their **best-contribution SKU** ($624/mo against assumed opex, versus $149 for the EPYC 4545P) and their **most stale**. That combination is the single strongest opening in this account.

5. **Do not pitch into the bottom of the range.** At $49/mo the Intel Special generates roughly $7/mo of contribution and a $168 ceiling even at twenty-four months. That tier runs on fully depreciated iron by design — their own clearance page says as much — and **no new-hardware proposal survives contact with it.**

**GAP — the custom-chassis premise underpinning any chassis-versus-board wallet split was NOT corroborated in this research pass.** The brief for this work stated that ReliableSite moved to their own custom chassis at roughly 50% higher density than 1U. **No public evidence of that transition was found in this pass, and it therefore cannot be dated from the catalogue.** What the record shows is the opposite continuity: the data-center page has carried the sentence *"Every dedicated server offered is physically racked using only server grade SuperMicro equipment in tier 3 and tier 4 facilities"* **unchanged in every archived capture from 2015-06-22 through 2025-12-08**, and it is still on the live page today, 2026-08-10. The clearance page has carried *"Each clearance server includes a quality server grade motherboard and SuperMicro branded rackable chassis with a 5+ year life expectancy"* unchanged from 2015-04-06 through 2024-07-15 and it too is live today. No mention of custom chassis, in-house chassis, or any density claim appears on the data-center page, the clearance page, the home page, the about page, the newsroom, PeeringDB, or in the Web Hosting Talk and LowEndTalk results returned in this pass. Either the transition is real but has left no public trace — entirely possible, since chassis choice is invisible to customers — or the premise is inaccurate. **It must be verified directly with the account before any pitch is built on it, because it determines whether the remaining wallet is motherboards only, or motherboards plus barebones plus chassis.** This does not overturn §6, which rests on a first-person CEO interview; it records that the catalogue and site evidence gathered for §§8–10 does not independently corroborate it ([data centre page](https://www.reliablesite.net/data-center/); [clearance servers page](https://www.reliablesite.net/dedicated-servers/clearance-servers/)).

**GAP — in-house build claim at reduced confidence.** Verbatim hardware sentences could not be extracted from the Amsterdam newsroom post. Direct retrieval of the page returned no such sentences. **Re-verify before quoting to a customer** — it matters, because it is the strongest available signal that they buy boards and barebones rather than integrated systems ([Amsterdam newsroom post](https://www.reliablesite.net/hosting-news/reliablesite-dedicated-servers-are-now-available-in-amsterdam/)).

## 14. Supermicro sales angle

### Classification: **confirmed incumbent — but in practice this is a chassis-loss defence in which the board-level socket is still open, plus a greenfield AI/GPU play.**

This is **not** a routine renewal and it is **not** a cold displacement. Supermicro is genuinely in this fleet, confirmed by three independent strands (§6). But the same CEO interview that confirms it is also the on-record account of **why they walked away from the vendor chassis**: MicroCloud replacement parts became unavailable, near-identical newer-generation parts would not fit, and the price was "2-3 times the cost", so he built his own ~50%-denser chassis — **while deliberately keeping it compatible with off-the-shelf motherboards and power supplies.**

That single design decision defines the entire honest wedge:

1. **Board and PSU, not chassis.** The chassis business is gone and should not be re-pitched first — re-opening it re-opens the grievance. What is still open, by the CEO's own published design intent, is the **motherboard and power supply socket**. **Who supplies the boards for the custom chassis is the single most commercially important unknown in this dossier**, and it is unanswerable from public sources.
2. **Parts availability and lead time is a live, first-hand pain point.** The CEO publicly stated an **"800% cost increase"** on specific SSD/RAM parts with **3+ month lead times** ([LowEndTalk](https://lowendtalk.com/discussion/218739/us-eu-dedicated-servers-epyc-4545p-ryzen-5800x-intel-core-i9-9900k-more-new-metal-benefits)). Long-life part availability and a stable supply commitment speak directly to the reason he left in the first place.
3. **The AI gap is real and published.** They market "GPU AI Dedicated Servers", position the EPYC 4545P for AI inferencing, and publish an AI-vision customer case study — with **one A10 (QTY 1) and a $99/month Quadro RTX 4000 add-on** behind it. A 4-GPU L40S / RTX PRO class node they can list, price and self-serve is the natural first GPU SKU, sized to a 1–2 rack single-socket operation, **not** an HGX/SXM scale-out pitch.
4. **They buy platforms Dell and HPE do not build.** The fleet is single-socket desktop/workstation silicon — AM5 Ryzen 9950X, LGA1700 i9 14900K, Threadripper 7995WX, EPYC 4545P. That is a structural product fit, and it is why there is no Dell or HPE incumbency to displace (both = no evidence found).
5. **Querétaro is the timing trigger.** The QRO region is publicly "coming soon" with the network test not yet live — a build-out in progress is the most likely near-term new-hardware purchase event in the account.

### The qualifying question to ask on first contact

> **"What motherboard and power supply are you using in your own chassis — and what platform is going into Querétaro?"**

Ask this and nothing else on the first call. It resolves the highest-value unknown in the dossier in one sentence, it acknowledges their custom chassis as theirs (which respects the design decision rather than attacking it), and it surfaces the live build-out. **Do not quote. Do not assume an incumbent. Do not open with "we know you run Supermicro"** — that is true of the fleet's history but is not confirmed at current-generation SKU level, and the CEO's own published account of leaving MicroCloud means a possessive opening will land badly.

### Rule 8 distributor caution — read before anyone dials
